SELENO Controller v0.8 Hardware Report
At a glance
SELENO v0.8 is an 85 × 50 mm, 4-layer telescope-mount controller. An ESP32 drives two TMC2209 stepper drivers from a 12 V input, takes commands from a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W over a 10-wire header, and runs a small LCD, five LEDs and seven buttons. The board passed its final pre-order review on 2026-10-15 with no blockers and is ready to order.

	There is no USB on the board. The v0.8 cost cut removed the USB hub, the CH340 USB-serial chip and the USB-A sockets; the ESP32 is now programmed through the Pi or through the PROG test pads, and the camera plugs into the Pi's own USB port. The IMU also moved off this board in v0.8 and connects through J3.
System architecture
The ESP32 is the hub: the Pi talks only to the ESP32, and the ESP32 talks to everything else. Power flows down the left column from the 12 V input, and +5V also leaves the board through J7 to run the Pi.

	Power
One 12 V input feeds three rails: +12V for the motor drivers, +5V at 5.12 V from a TPS5430 buck rated 3 A, and +3.3V from an AMS1117 LDO fed from +5V. The buck has room for the Pi, its camera and the whole 3.3 V rail at once (about 2.1 A worst-case peak against 3 A). The input survives reverse polarity and clamps surges at about 24 V.

	—
	How Q1 protects the board: with correct polarity, current first flows through Q1's body diode, so the source rises to the input voltage. R21 holds the gate near 0 V, the channel turns on, and the drop falls to about 80 mV at 2 A. Above about 10 V of input, D6 conducts and holds V_GS at −V_Z (9.4–10.6 V), which keeps at least 1.4 V of margin to the ±12 V rating, even while D7 is clamping a surge. With the input reversed, the body diode is reverse-biased and V_GS is near 0 V, so Q1 stays off and nothing flows. F1 does not trip in that case.
Usable input range: about 7–15 V, with 12 V as the design point. The low end is the buck's limit (5 V regulation holds down to about 6.7 V at 3 A, from its 87 % maximum duty cycle). The high end is F1's 15 V rating and D7's stand-off. Do not use a 19–24 V adapter: D7 would clamp it and F1 would trip above its rating.

* Duty cycle about 0.46, so the inductor ripple is about 0.4 A peak-to-peak (13 % of 3 A). The peak current at a 3 A load is about 3.2 A: under L2's 4.9 A saturation current and under U6's 4.0 A minimum current limit.

Power-up sequence
1. 12 V arrives. Q1 turns on through its body diode, and D7 and C1 absorb the plug-in transient.
2. U6 starts once VIN passes 5.3 V. Its 8 ms soft-start ramps +5V, which also powers the Pi through J7.
3. U9 follows +5V up, so +3.3V settles within about 10 ms of plug-in.
4. The ESP32's EN rises through R6 10 kΩ / C7 1 µF (τ = 10 ms). The chip leaves reset at 0.75 × VDD, about 14 ms after +3.3V is up; Espressif recommends exactly this RC.
5. The MCP23017's RESET rises through R19 10 kΩ / C33 10 µF (τ = 100 ms). Wait at least 200 ms after power-up before the first I²C access to it.
6. R35/R36 hold both TMC2209 ENN pins high, so the motors stay unpowered until the firmware pulls 1EN/2EN low.
Microcontroller core
U5, an ESP32-WROOM-32UE, runs the whole board: it talks to the Pi on UART0, drives both motors, reads the buttons through the MCP23017 and draws on the LCD. 21 of the module's 26 usable GPIOs are assigned; IO25, IO26, IO36 and the strap pins IO2 and IO12 are left free on purpose.
The module
* ESP32-D0WD-V3 chip: dual-core Xtensa LX6 up to 240 MHz, 448 KB ROM, 520 KB SRAM, 16 KB RTC SRAM. Wi-Fi 802.11 b/g/n and Bluetooth 4.2 (BR/EDR + LE).
* The 40 MHz crystal and the 4, 8 or 16 MB QSPI flash are inside the module. Flash pins GPIO6–GPIO11 are internal, so module pins 17–22 and 32 are no-connects.
* Supply 3.0–3.6 V; Espressif requires a supply that can deliver at least 0.5 A. Operating range −40 to 85 °C.
* The 32UE has no PCB antenna. It has an IPEX socket for an external 2.4 GHz antenna, at the module end that faces the right-hand board edge. Fit the antenna before enabling Wi-Fi or Bluetooth.
* Buy the -N4, -N8 or -N16 variant only. The -NxR2 variants carry PSRAM that takes over IO16, which is motor 1 DIR on this board.
* Footprint RF_Module:ESP32-WROOM-32UE at (70.4, 16.8) mm, rotated −90°. Its centre pad (pin 39) has 12 thermal vias, opened to 0.3 mm drills, connected solid to GND. An F.Cu rule area keeps copper out from under the module. Soldering the centre pad by hand is optional; Espressif calls it "not a must".
* Decoupling: C11 100 nF right at pin 2 (3V3) and C43 22 µF about 4.5 mm away. That matches Espressif's reference of 22 µF + 0.1 µF at the 3V3 pin.
Reset and boot straps
EN (pin 3, net ESP32EN) has R6 10 kΩ to 3.3 V and C7 1 µF to GND, Espressif's recommended RC. Three things can pull it low: the off-board RESET button on SW7 (top-right corner, silk "RST"), Q3 from the Pi's auto-reset pair, and test pad TP18. GPIO0 (pin 25) has only the chip's internal pull-up; Q2 pulls it low for download mode, and TP19 gives hand access.

	Notes for the firmware
* Peripheral mapping. UART0 on IO1/IO3 is the Pi link. The drivers' single-wire UART uses a second UART routed through the GPIO matrix (RX IO19, TX IO18). I²C goes on IO33 (SDA) / IO32 (SCL), not on Arduino's default 21/22, which are motor pins here. The LCD SPI clock and data sit on the HSPI IO_MUX pins (IO14/IO13), so they can run at full SPI speed. The backlight takes an LEDC PWM channel on IO5.
* Drive strength. Outputs default to about 20 mA drive; the maximum setting is about 40 mA. Weak internal pulls are about 45 kΩ (about 75 µA).
* IO39 glitch. When the SAR ADCs or the RTC amplifier power up (Wi-Fi power-save can do this), Espressif says GPIO36 and GPIO39 "will be pulled down for approximately 80 ns". EXP_INT is active-low, so firmware must treat an EXP_INT edge as a hint: read the MCP23017's INTF/INTCAP registers and ignore the event if nothing changed.
* Spare resources: IO25 and IO26 (DAC-capable), IO36 (input-only), IO2 and IO12 (straps; outputs only after boot, with care), MCP23017 GPA3–GPA6 (no pads), J7 pin 9 (Pi GPIO24, unconnected), and both TMC2209 INDEX outputs (unconnected).
Raspberry Pi link (J7)
J7 is a 1×10 2.54 mm header wired one-to-one to the even row of the Pi Zero 2 W header (J7 pin n = Pi pin 2n). Over those 10 wires the board powers the Pi, the Pi and ESP32 share a 3.3 V UART, and two Pi GPIOs can reset the ESP32 into its bootloader. The Pi's own 3V3 (pin 1) is deliberately not connected, so the two 3.3 V regulators never fight.

The cable is straight-through: each J7 pin lands directly under its Pi pin, and the odd row (including the Pi's 3V3 on pin 1) is never used.
J7 sits on the top-left edge: pin 1 (square pad) at board (8.4, 2.2) mm, pin 10 at (31.3, 2.2) mm, silk "PI: EVEN ROW, PIN 2 ... PIN 20". Both sides use 3.3 V logic, so no level shifting is needed.
Power to the Pi
The Pi runs from the board's +5V (5.12 V nominal) through header pins 2 and 4. That bypasses the input protection on the Pi's micro-USB power socket, so the board's buck, PTC and TVS are the Pi's only protection. Four vias feed the In2 +5V island that J7 sits on. A Pi Zero 2 W plus a camera on its USB port fits easily in the buck's 3 A budget (see Power).
* Never plug the Pi's own USB power in while J7 is connected. Both supplies would drive the same 5 V rail.
* Keep the cable short (20–30 cm). With two 5 V wires and three ground wires, a 1 A Pi load loses roughly 50 mV across a 20 cm Dupont cable (estimate). The Pi flags under-voltage at about 4.63 V, so there is plenty of margin from 5.12 V.
Cable and the two ways to get it wrong
Use a 10-way female-female Dupont ribbon: a 1×10 housing on the board end and, ideally, a 2×10 housing on the Pi end with only the even-row positions populated. The 2×10 housing can only go on one way and one row. A 10-pin KF2510 or Molex KK 254 locking header fits J7's holes if you want a latching plug.
Mistake
	What happens
	Plug on the Pi's odd row
	J7 pin 1 (5 V) lands on Pi pin 1 (3V3) and pin 2 (5 V) on GPIO2. That back-drives the Pi's 3.3 V rail with 5 V and kills the Pi.
	1×10 plug reversed on the board
	+5V meets the GND wire (a dead short; the buck goes into hiccup), and 5 V lands on Pi GPIO24, which is not 5 V-tolerant.
	Mark pin 1 (5 V) on the cable housing, and check the cable with a meter before the first power-up.
UART link
The ESP32's UART0 is wired straight to the Pi's GPIO14/15. On the Pi Zero 2 W, the good PL011 UART normally serves Bluetooth, and GPIO14/15 get the mini UART, whose baud rate follows the VideoCore core clock. The current host software runs at 921600 baud, so give GPIO14/15 the PL011 and free them from the Linux console:
1. sudo raspi-config → Interface Options → Serial Port → login shell over serial: No; serial port hardware: Yes.
2. Add dtoverlay=disable-bt to /boot/firmware/config.txt (/boot/config.txt on older OS releases), then run sudo systemctl disable hciuart and reboot.
3. The link is now /dev/serial0, which points to /dev/ttyAMA0. Add your user to the dialout group.
The PL011's default 48 MHz clock reaches 921600 baud with about 0.2 % error. The ESP32 ROM prints its boot banner at 115200 baud after every reset, so the host should flush its input after resetting the ESP32.

Peripheral subsystems
Four blocks hang off the ESP32: two TMC2209 stepper drivers on +12V, an MCP23017 that handles the buttons and LEDs, an ST7735S LCD on SPI, and an I²C cable to the IMU board. The drivers come up disabled, and the shared driver UART must set the microstep resolution: out of the box the two drivers disagree (1/8 versus 1/32).

The shared single-wire UART. IO19 (RX) connects straight to both PDN_UART pins; IO18 (TX) drives the same net through R16 1 kΩ. That is Trinamic's recommended single-wire hookup: a replying driver can override the idle-high TX through the resistor. The ESP32 hears its own transmissions echoed back and must discard them (the TMCStepper library does this).
* Baud rate 9600 to 500 kbaud, detected automatically from the sync nibble. 115200 is a safe choice.
* Write datagram (8 bytes): 0x05, address (0 or 1), register | 0x80, 4 data bytes MSB-first, CRC.
* Read request (4 bytes): 0x05, address, register, CRC. The reply is 8 bytes with address 0xFF.
* CRC-8 (polynomial x⁸ + x² + x + 1, initial value 0), applied LSB to MSB over every byte.
* With two drivers on one wire, set SENDDELAY (NODECONF, 0x03) to at least 2 on both. IFCNT (0x02) counts accepted writes, so read it back to confirm a write landed.
* Check IOIN (0x06): its VERSION byte reads 0x21 on a TMC2209.
First-time configuration per driver (registers from the datasheet):
1. GCONF (0x00): set pdn_disable (bit 6) and mstep_reg_select (bit 7); keep I_scale_analog (bit 0) = 1 and multistep_filt (bit 8) = 1. Bit 2 en_SpreadCycle = 0 keeps StealthChop.
2. CHOPCONF (0x6C): set MRES (bits 27:24) the same on both drivers, for example 4 = 1/16 or 5 = 1/8, and keep intpol (bit 28) = 1 for 256-step interpolation.
3. IHOLD_IRUN (0x10): for example IRUN 23, IHOLD 11, IHOLDDELAY 6.
4. Optional: TPWMTHRS (0x13) to switch to SpreadCycle at speed; SGTHRS (0x40) + TCOOLTHRS (0x14) for StallGuard on DIAG (sensorless homing).
5. Only then pull ENN low.
Heat. The datasheet puts the driver's loss at about 1.4 W at 1.0 A RMS and 2.8 W at 1.4 A RMS, and quotes 30 K/W only on a 70 × 133 mm 1 oz 2s2p board. This board has 0.5 oz inner copper and no outer pours, so expect worse. At the full 1.35 A the drivers will likely reach the 143 °C shutdown (pre-warning at 120 °C, both readable in DRV_STATUS 0x6F). The plan: 9 × 9 mm stick-on heatsinks on U7/U8, IRUN about 23 (1.0 A), a reduced hold current, and solid thermal vias (done in the final review, fix F1).
Motors. The motors are SIMAX3D NEMA17s rated 1.5 A, with 6-pin JST-PH sockets. A standard PH2.0-6P to XH2.54-4P stepper cable plugs into J1/J2: pins 1–2 are coil A (OA1, OA2) and pins 3–4 coil B (OB1, OB2). Check each cable first: XH pins 1–2 and 3–4 should each read one coil, about 2–3 Ω. Never plug or unplug a motor while the board has 12 V on it; an open coil under current can destroy the driver's bridge.

Programming and firmware
The ESP32 is flashed over its UART0 ROM bootloader. That works three ways: from the Pi over J7 (the normal path), from a 3.3 V USB-serial adapter on the PROG pads (the bench path), or by hand with the IO0 pad and the RESET button. The existing firmware on the Client/Rpi5 branch builds as-is, but its pin numbers come from the old dev-kit wiring and must be updated for this board (table at the end).
Toolchain
* PlatformIO, platform = espressif32, board = esp32dev, framework = arduino. The esp32dev profile suits the WROOM-32UE and assumes 4 MB of flash, which is safe on -N4, -N8 and -N16 modules.
* Libraries already used: teemuatlut/TMCStepper@^0.7.3, bblanchon/ArduinoJson@^7.2.1.
* The host link is UART0 (Serial in Arduino), so add -DCORE_DEBUG_LEVEL=0 to the build flags to keep ESP-IDF log lines out of the JSON protocol.
* Arduino flash layout, for flashing by hand: bootloader at 0x1000, partition table at 0x8000, boot_app0 at 0xE000, application at 0x10000. PlatformIO writes them to .pio/build/<env>/.
* Building on the Pi Zero 2 W (512 MB RAM) is slow. Building on a PC and copying the four .bin files to the Pi works well.

Path A: flash from the Pi over J7
esptool can't toggle Pi GPIOs, so a wrapper puts the ESP32 into download mode through GPIO18/GPIO23 (the truth table is in Raspberry Pi link), runs esptool with --before no_reset --after no_reset, then resets the ESP32 into its new application. It needs the Pi UART set up as described there and pip install esptool gpiozero.
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Flash the SELENO v0.8 ESP32 from the Pi over J7 (GPIO18 = DTR, GPIO23 = RTS)."""
import subprocess
import sys
import time

from gpiozero import DigitalOutputDevice

PORT = "/dev/serial0"
dtr = DigitalOutputDevice(18, initial_value=False)  # J7 pin 6 -> Q3 base, Q2 emitter
rts = DigitalOutputDevice(23, initial_value=False)  # J7 pin 8 -> Q2 base, Q3 emitter


def lines(d, r, hold_s):
   dtr.value, rts.value = d, r
   time.sleep(hold_s)


def enter_bootloader():
   lines(1, 0, 0.10)  # EN low: hold the ESP32 in reset
   lines(0, 1, 0.10)  # EN released while IO0 is low -> ROM download mode
   lines(0, 0, 0.01)  # both transistors off; the ROM waits for esptool


def run_app():
   lines(1, 0, 0.10)  # reset with IO0 high
   lines(0, 0, 0.01)


if __name__ == "__main__":
   enter_bootloader()
   try:
       rc = subprocess.call(["esptool.py", "--chip", "esp32", "--port", PORT,
                             "--baud", "921600", "--before", "no_reset",
                             "--after", "no_reset", *sys.argv[1:]])
   finally:
       run_app()
   sys.exit(rc)
Examples:
* Check the link: python3 seleno_flash.py flash_id
* Flash a build: python3 seleno_flash.py write_flash -z 0x1000 bootloader.bin 0x8000 partitions.bin 0xe000 boot_app0.bin 0x10000 firmware.bin
* esptool v5 renames the command to esptool and prefers hyphens (--before no-reset, write-flash).
* PlatformIO on the Pi also works: call enter_bootloader(), run pio run -t upload --upload-port /dev/serial0, then call run_app(). PlatformIO's own DTR/RTS reset does nothing on /dev/serial0 because those modem lines aren't wired to pins.
Path B: USB-serial adapter on the PROG pads
Use an adapter with 3.3 V logic that brings out DTR and RTS (CP2102N, CH340 or FT232R boards). Its DTR/RTS drive the same Q2/Q3 pair the CH340 used to, so esptool's automatic reset works unchanged.

Path C: by hand
Hold IO0 low (tweezers from TP19 to TP14 GND), tap RESET (SW7, or TP18 to GND), release IO0, then flash on any UART path with --before no_reset. Tap RESET again to run.

Bring-up code: the drivers
Configure both drivers before enabling either one. The microstep setting must be written over UART, because the straps leave U7 at 1/8 and U8 at 1/32.
#include <TMCStepper.h>
#include "seleno_v08_pins.h"

TMC2209Stepper drv1(&Serial2, TMC_RSENSE, 0);  // U7, MS1 = MS2 = 0
TMC2209Stepper drv2(&Serial2, TMC_RSENSE, 1);  // U8, MS1 = 1

static void setupDriver(TMC2209Stepper &d) {
 d.begin();
 d.pdn_disable(true);       // PDN_UART is the UART, not standstill power-down
 d.mstep_reg_select(true);  // microsteps from MRES, not the MS pins
 d.I_scale_analog(true);    // VREF = 3.3 V gives full scale
 d.toff(4);                 // chopper on
 d.microsteps(16);          // identical on both drivers
 d.rms_current(1000, 0.5);  // 1.0 A RMS run, 0.5 A hold
 d.en_spreadCycle(false);   // StealthChop
 d.pwm_autoscale(true);
}

void setupMotors() {
 pinMode(PIN_M1_EN, OUTPUT); digitalWrite(PIN_M1_EN, HIGH);  // drivers off
 pinMode(PIN_M2_EN, OUTPUT); digitalWrite(PIN_M2_EN, HIGH);
 Serial2.begin(115200, SERIAL_8N1, PIN_TMC_RX, PIN_TMC_TX);
 setupDriver(drv1);
 setupDriver(drv2);
 if (drv1.test_connection() == 0 && drv2.test_connection() == 0) {
   digitalWrite(PIN_M1_EN, LOW);  // only enable a configured driver
   digitalWrite(PIN_M2_EN, LOW);
 }
}
Read drvN.version() (expect 0x21) and drvN.DRV_STATUS() during bring-up; DRV_STATUS shows over-temperature, short-to-ground and open-load flags.
Bring-up code: buttons and LEDs
#include <Wire.h>
#include "seleno_v08_pins.h"

static void mcpWrite(uint8_t reg, uint8_t val) {
 Wire.beginTransmission(MCP23017_ADDR);
 Wire.write(reg);
 Wire.write(val);
 Wire.endTransmission();
}

static uint8_t mcpRead(uint8_t reg) {
 Wire.beginTransmission(MCP23017_ADDR);
 Wire.write(reg);
 Wire.endTransmission(false);
 Wire.requestFrom(MCP23017_ADDR, (uint8_t)1);
 return Wire.read();
}

void setupExpander() {
 Wire.begin(PIN_SDA, PIN_SCL, 400000);
 mcpWrite(0x0A, 0x44);  // IOCON: MIRROR, open-drain INT
 mcpWrite(0x00, 0x7C);  // IODIRA: GPA2-6 in; GPA0, GPA1, GPA7 out
 mcpWrite(0x01, 0x1F);  // IODIRB: GPB0-4 in; GPB5-7 out
 mcpWrite(0x0C, 0x7C);  // GPPUA
 mcpWrite(0x0D, 0x1F);  // GPPUB
 mcpWrite(0x04, 0x04);  // GPINTENA: SELECT
 mcpWrite(0x05, 0x1F);  // GPINTENB: RETURN, TOP, BOTTOM, LEFT, RIGHT
 mcpRead(0x10);         // INTCAPA: clear anything pending
 mcpRead(0x11);         // INTCAPB
 pinMode(PIN_EXP_INT, INPUT);  // IO39 has no internal pull-up; R38 provides it
}

	Procedure
1. Inspect the JLC assembly under magnification. Check pin 1 of U1, U6, U7, U8 and U9, the orientation of Q1–Q3, and the cathode bands of D6, D7 and D8 against the silk. Look for bridges on the three QFNs (U1, U7, U8).
2. Check for shorts with a meter, unpowered: TP1–TP2, TP3–TP4 and TP5–TP6 must not read as a short (well above 100 Ω; the readings climb as the capacitors charge).
3. Fit P1 and C1 (C1's "+" is pad 1, the +12V side; the filled half of its silk circle is GND), and nothing else that's hand-soldered yet.
4. First power: 12 V with the supply limited to 100 mA. Draw should be tens of mA, and nothing should get warm. Measure the three rails against the table. Scope the switch node and +5V if you have a scope.
5. Optional reverse-polarity test: with the same 100 mA limit, swap the leads. Draw should be essentially zero and every rail 0 V.
6. Fit U5 (ESP32), its antenna, and SW7. Power up again with a 300 mA limit. TP18 (EN) and TP19 (IO0) should both read 3.3 V.
7. Talk to the ESP32 over the PROG pads (Path B in Programming and firmware): esptool.py --port COMx flash_id should report an ESP32-D0WD-V3 and the flash size you bought (4, 8 or 16 MB).
8. Fit the remaining hand-soldered parts: D1–D5 (the cathode is the closed end of the silk bracket, toward the board edge), R25–R28, and the J1, J2, J3, J7, S1 and SW1–SW6 connectors.
9. I²C and expander: flash a test sketch and scan the bus. Expect 0x20 (U1), plus 0x4A with the IMU board connected. Light each LED in turn. Press each button and check that TP33 drops and INTF shows the right bit.
10. LCD: initialise it, fill the screen red, green and blue, and sweep the backlight PWM.
11. Driver UART, motors still unplugged: read IOIN from addresses 0 and 1. Both should return VERSION 0x21, and IFCNT should count up after each write. Confirm both ENN pads still read 3.3 V.
12. First motor run: power off and check each motor cable with a meter (XH pins 1–2 and 3–4 each read one coil, about 2–3 Ω). Plug the motors in, then power on. Configure both drivers with a low current (IRUN about 8–11, roughly 0.4–0.5 A), enable, and step slowly. Check direction, smoothness, and that DIAG stays low. Then raise IRUN to 23 (1.0 A), fit the heatsinks, and run for 10 minutes while watching DRV_STATUS for the 120 °C pre-warning.
13. The Pi, last: with 12 V off, connect the J7 cable and confirm with a meter that J7 pin 1 reaches Pi pin 2. Power on. The Pi should boot from the board, +5V should stay above 4.9 V through the Pi's boot, and vcgencmd get_throttled should return throttled=0x0 (no under-voltage). Set up the UART, run seleno_flash.py flash_id, then test the JSON link at 921600 baud.
14. Full load: with Wi-Fi active, both motors running, the Pi and its camera attached, measure the 12 V input current (expect 1.5–2.5 A) and touch-check U6, D8, L2 and U9. Warm is fine; too hot to hold is not.
PCB layout
The board is 85.0 × 50.0 mm, 1.6 mm thick, with four copper layers and every part on the top side. In1 is a solid ground plane, In2 carries the power rails, and the two outer layers carry the signals with no copper pours. DRC is clean: 0 errors, 0 unconnected items, schematic parity 0.

Power routing
* 12 V in: P1 → F1 on a 1.5 mm track → Q1 on 1.0 mm → 3 vias into the In2 +12V island. The island feeds U6 (2 vias at C35), both drivers' VS pins, C47 and C1. P1, C1 and J7 power pins have solid (unrelieved) plane connections.
* 5 V buck (laid out like TI's Figure 7-12): C35 sits right against VIN and the PowerPAD ground. D8 sits above U6, with its anode dropping into the PowerPAD through a 1.2 mm trace and 2 GND vias. The switch node is a 1.0 mm strip from U6's PH pin over D8 to L2. VSENSE runs straight down into the compensation row. The +5V output reaches the In2 +5V island through 4 vias (2 added in the final review, fix F2).
* 3.3 V: U9 is fed on B.Cu from a +5V via at (14.6, 23.3) to its input capacitor C50. Its tab feeds the 3V3 plane through the F.Cu spreader and 4 vias.
* Ground: every ground pin has its own via into In1, with 70 GND vias in total. The drivers' sense resistors have separate GND vias, as the TMC2209 datasheet asks (no shared return between BRA and BRB).

Editing the board
The installed board includes hand routing and scripted fixes that the review/pcb_build/ scripts don't reproduce. Edit SELENO.kicad_pcb directly in KiCad; never regenerate it from the scripts. Board-local coordinates used in the logs are KiCad absolute coordinates minus (76.5, 45.5). Close KiCad before any scripted edit, and check for ~*.lck lock files.

In Git Bash, pass C:/... style paths to kicad-cli; with MSYS_NO_PATHCONV=1 a /c/... path is taken literally. The generator refuses to write files if a part is missing, has the wrong value or sits on the bottom side.
Design history
The design went from its first commit in May 2026 to an order-ready board on 2026-10-15. Most of the change happened in three days of review (13–15 October): the schematic was fixed and split into sheets, the board was rebuilt twice, and the USB hub and the AP62300 bucks were cut for cost. The final-review fixes are in commit 5733ccd (v0.8.4) on branch hardware; the project rename that followed is not committed yet.
